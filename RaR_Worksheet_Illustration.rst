Worksheet Illustration
==================

This document illustrates how a practitioner would ideally respond to our worksheet for a hypothetical HCI project.

Hypothetical HCI Project:
------------------------
Meena, an HCI researcher, is building a content moderation platform to explore two questions: (a) whether AI-assisted annotation improves the process, and (b) how AI influences human decision-making. Content moderation and annotation have received significant attention in HCI, as they involve both human-centered concerns and the sociotechnical implications of system design. Building on this literature, Meena develops an interactive application with three components: (a) Input reasoning box: annotators provide free-text explanations for their decisions on whether a text is toxic, (b) AI-suggested reasoning widget: Displays reasoning generated by an AI to support the annotator’s decision, (c) Post-reason widget: Shows the annotator’s own reasoning from similar texts they annotated earlier in the session. Her system handles texts in English and Tamil, including code-switched examples. Three LLMs independently support different tasks in the system: LLM-A: Rephrases the annotator’s input reasoning for proofreading; LLM-B: Generates new reasoning suggestions; LLM-C: Retrieves similar texts and past reasoning from the session. Meena designs two user experiments. In the first, participants interact with only the input reasoning box and the past-reason widget. In the second, the AI-generated reasoning widget is added to study its influence on decision-making. In both experiments, she collects survey data to quantify user experiences and conducts semi-structured interviews to gather qualitative insights.

Reasoning Identification:
------------------------
**I.1** Does the task your system is supposed to do involve some form of reasoning as per your interpretation? Describe the task and then explain why the task be interpreted specifically as reasoning.

**I.2** Does the expected LLM response (at inference) involve reasoning as per your interpretation? Describe the LLM response and then explain why the response be interpreted specifically as reasoning.

**I.3** Does your system consist of an explicitly trained reasoning language model? Describe what characteristics or behaviors of the model lead you to that view and then explain why those aspects indicate whether it is a reasoning model or not.

  *Meena begins by assessing whether her LLMs are actually performing reasoning. Based on her review of the literature, she defines reasoning as the process of drawing logical conclusions by breaking a problem into smaller subproblems. She identifies that the second task---where LLM-B generates reasons---involves reasoning (I.1), and that its output clearly reflects reasoning (I.2). She determines that the other two tasks do not require reasoning: LLM-A primarily performs pattern matching, and LLM-C handles semantic similarity computations, as confirmed by prior studies. Wanting more flexibility in her models, she considers using LLaMA-3.3-70B, an open-source LLM. While it is not inherently a reasoning-focused model (RLM) and does not generate intermediate <think> tokens, it offers strong multilingual performance, making it suitable for her platform.*

Reasoning Framing:
------------------
**F.1** Is the reasoning process structured in an orderly step-by-step manner? Justify why this is necessary.

**F.2** Is the reasoning process structured in stages or phases (where each stage may have its own sub-task) with a degree of order between them? Justify why this framing is necessary for your task.

**F.3** How are individual steps or stages or phases organized and interacted with one another? Do the steps branch and/or merge? Justify why your desired mode of organization is necessary for your task.

**F.4** Does a step or stage or phase include multiple reasoning-related sub-tasks? If so, explain why they are necessary for your task and how they are related to LLM reasoning.

**F.5** List down all possible interpretations that a reasoning step or stage or phase can refer to in your application. Explain why you interpret them that way and how they connect to your system's behavior and goals.

**F.6** Can the terminal state of your reasoning task that the LLM(s) is(are) performing have one or more than one possible conclusions? Explain why this multiplicity arises or why it may be necessary.

**F.7** Can each of the possible conclusion of your reasoning task be objectively understood or are they open to subjective interpretations? Describe what makes your preferred interpretation reasonable and why that matters.

**F.8** Is your interpreted reasoning behavior at inference expected to follow some structure or form? Justify why a structure is (or is not) required and explain the level of explicitness in the structure, if applicable.

**F.9** Can the reasoning behavior at inference be structured in ways other than by separating it into multiple parts or sub-tasks? If so, explain why this might be preferred in your case.

  *Examining F.1—F.3, Meena realizes that she could explore alternative reasoning frames beyond a standard CoT approach. From her initial review, questions F.6—F.7 make her consider that LLMs might arrive at different conclusions for the same input. Based on this, she decides to adopt a tree structure and develops a new plan for LLM-B to explore two distinct reasoning paths for each text. In the first path, she implements a stricter interpretation of Meta's hate speech policy (https://transparency.meta.com/policies/community-standards/hateful-conduct/), condensing it into clear instructions. She instructs the model to start by defining what constitutes a ``safe'' text under this standard and then reason step by step to identify potential toxicity.* 
                                                                                                                                                                                                                     
  *In the second path, she takes a more open-ended approach: first considering any potentially useful intent behind the text, then assessing toxicity without applying a specific standard. When both paths generate multiple reasons at their terminal states, she instructs LLM-B to select overlapping reasons to produce a final consolidated list. If there are no overlaps—or if one path labels the text as toxic and the other as non-toxic—she instructs the model to classify it as non-toxic, using reasons from the non-toxic path. She believes this approach will yield richer and more diverse reasoning that annotators might otherwise overlook. Through this process, Meena identifies that one step in the first path involves multiple sub-tasks (F.4)—specifically, checking toxicity against a detailed standards list—and may involve multiple interpretations (F.5). Considering F.4 and F.5 prompts her to refine her instructions to minimize ambiguity.*


Reasoning Execution
-------------------

Inference Scaling
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

**E.1** Does your task rely solely on prompting techniques? If so, what strengths and capabilities of LLMs convince you that this is sufficient?

**E.2** Did you include any domain knowledge in your prompt(s)? If not, justify why this is not required or explain how you interpret LLMs can incorporate the necessary knowledge.

**E.3** How did you choose in-context examples or demonstrations for your task? Justify why these samples will help LLMs to respond better.

**E.4** Were your final prompt(s) evolved over several iterations? Explain what changes were necessary and why they were important.

**E.5** Do you explicitly introduce the reasoning frame, required for your task, in the prompt? Justify your stance.

**E.6** If you considered some form for finetuning LLMs but did not implement it, explain what made you not implement and why this is acceptable.

  *Meena begins by translating her requirements into prompt instructions, leveraging the improved ability of LLMs to follow human guidance (E.1). She chooses not to include demonstrations in her prompts (E.3), concerned that longer context lengths might reduce performance. She iterates on the prompt for several example texts, making minor formatting adjustments based on observed responses (E.4).*
                                                        
Reasoning Finetuning
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

**E.7** To what extent do the contents of the finetuning datasets reflect the kinds of responses your system is expected to produce at inference? Explain why this alignment is necessary.

**E.8** If the finetuning datasets are not specifically developed for your task, justify why task-specific datasets are not used.

**E.9** Are the ways reasoning is structured (steps, stages, or phases) and organized (in chains, trees, or graphs) in finetuning datasets similar to how your system is expected to respond at inference? Explain why the similarities and differences are acceptable.

**E.10** Does your finetuning datasets contain flawed reasoning patterns (as per your task)? If so, explain how this is (or is not) a problem; if not, justify your stance.

**E.11** Does your system's performance on tasks other than (or somewhat related to) yours change due to the finetuning? If this aspect is insignificant, explain why.

  *During this initial testing, she notices that LLM-C does not meet her expectations, particularly when toxicity appears in code-switched English-Tamil texts. To address this, she collects a toxicity dataset from publicly available local media sources and decides to fine-tune the model on this dataset. Although the dataset was not originally designed for her task (E.8), she edits it with her co-authors to better reflect the types of responses the model would encounter at inference (E.7). She then qualitatively validates the fine-tuned model on both edge cases and prototypical examples, confirming that it performs better than relying on prompting alone (E.11).*

Reasoning Evaluation
--------------------

**V.1** Do you conduct a preliminary evaluation of LLMs' reasoning abilities prior to applying them for your task? If not, explain why this is not necessary.

**V.2** Justify why LLM reasoning is necessary or beneficial to your target user?

**V.3** How do you ensure and assess whether your users consider LLMs' reasoning behavior (towards a desired terminal state) when determining your system's utility? Explain why your method works.

**V.4** If your evaluation of LLM reasoning is predominantly qualitative, justify why this is necessary and/or sufficient? If your evaluation involves quantitative methods, are they against ground truth values or user feedback? Justify why you chose one over the other, if applicable.

**V.5** Do you refer to LLMs' benchmark performance to interpret their reasoning ability? If so, justify why these established results are sufficient for your task.

**V.6** How aligned are the modes of reasoning in these benchmarks with respect to your task? Describe the level of alignment necessary for your task and explain why this is (in)sufficient.

**V.7** Do your benchmarks contain intermediate reasoning steps? Justify why this does (not) affect your interpretation of LLM reasoning required for your task.

**V.8** How do you justify that the LLMs you use did not memorize the benchmark test datasets?

  *Before conducting the final user study, Meena evaluates LLM-C's reasoning abilities using both qualitative and quantitative approaches. She tests several example cases qualitatively and compares the model's final outputs—labeled as toxic or non-toxic—against ground truth data form the media sources (V.1, V.4). Her evaluation of the platform combines qualitative interviews with quantitative surveys, directly assessing the extent to which LLM reasoning is beneficial or influential for data annotators (V.3).*
